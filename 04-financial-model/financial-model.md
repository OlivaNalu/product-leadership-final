> **The case in one paragraph:** What was missing to strengthen the case was actual numbers, I have added assumptions and figures that if true allow us to fund the case on condition. Meridian Foundations: Foreman-Facing Mobile App Business Case The strategic bet. We bet that a dedicated mobile app will make it faster and easier for foremen to capture, communicate, and resolve on-site issues, increasing sustained field adoption and making Meridian more valuable to enterprise customers. Better field adoption should improve project visibility and strengthen customer retention, generating incremental contribution that exceeds the cost of building and maintaining the app. The assumptions. The case assumes that 65% of eligible field users adopt the app, that better adoption reduces annual customer churn from 12% to 10%, and that retained customers generate an 80% contribution margin. The most critical assumption is the two-percentage-point churn reduction: if adoption does not translate into better retention, the financial case weakens substantially. These assumptions must be validated against actual Meridian customer data. The expected return. Assuming 800 eligible enterprise accounts, an average annual contract value of $75,000, and 65% adoption, approximately 520 accounts would adopt the app. A two-percentage-point reduction in annual churn would represent approximately 10 fewer lost accounts per year, equivalent to $780,000 in retained ARR and $624,000 in annual contribution. Against a $450,000 initial build cost and $120,000 in annual operating costs, the model projects approximately $750,000 in cumulative net contribution over three years, with payback around 18 months after launch. These estimates assume a gradual rollout and an incremental retention effect; they are projections, not proven returns. The kill criterion. By the end of Q2, after a 12-week pilot, at least 60% of invited foremen must actively complete core issue-reporting workflows each week, and issue reporting must be at least 20% faster than with the current workflow. If either threshold is missed, stop further investment in the separate app and reallocate Q3 engineering capacity to improving field workflows in the existing app. Meeting the thresholds permits further validation, not automatic approval of the full rollout. Recommendation: FUND WITH ONE CONDITION. Approve a capped pilot, with full investment conditional on demonstrating sustained adoption and credible evidence that it can improve retention. Before committing the full budget, validate the assumed two-percentage-point churn reduction using Meridian’s actual customer data and a credible comparison group. The business case is attractive if that effect is real; without it, the projected return is not sufficiently reliable to justify the investment.

## 2. Kill criteria

> **Kill criterion:** Kill criterion: - Metric: Percentage of pilot foremen who actively use the app each week to complete core issue-reporting workflows. - Threshold: At least 60% weekly active adoption among invited pilot foremen. - Timeline: By the end of Q2, after 12 weeks of pilot use. - Financial consequence: If adoption remains below 60%, stop further investment in the separate app and reallocate Q3 engineering capacity to improving field workflows within the existing Meridian app. Any exception requires explicit approval from the accountable product leader.

## Lab notes

**Make your evaluation and funding decision**

- **What assumption is doing the most work? If this number is 20-30% off, what changes?:** The assumption doing the most work is that the AI estimation feature causes a sustained increase in Standard-to-Enterprise conversion from 6% to 8% within two quarters and that the 8% conversion applies to 400 eligible Standard accounts in Year 1. The conversion assumption does the most work because it’s the link between building the feature and making money from it. In simple terms: - The feature costs $180,000 to build. - You assume it will increase the share of Standard customers who upgrade to Enterprise from 6% to 8%. - That means 8 extra customers out of 400 upgrade because of the feature. - Those 8 customers generate $224,000 in additional annual revenue. The problem is that if conversion only improves from 6% to 7%, you get just 4 extra upgrades instead of 8. Your expected incremental ARR falls from $224,000 to $112,000. So, a small change in the conversion assumption cuts the expected return in half.
- **What is the structural problem in this case? Look past the headline numbers for something that does not hold up on closer inspection.:** The structural problem is that the case treats all Enterprise upgrades as if they were caused by the new feature, rather than separating upgrades that would happen anyway from genuinely incremental upgrades. The numbers expose this as follows: - 6% baseline conversion means 24 of 400 Standard accounts would upgrade anyway. - 8% target conversion means 32 accounts upgrade. - The feature’s assumed contribution is therefore only 8 additional upgrades, not 32. At $28,000 per contract, the case claims $896,000 in incremental ARR, but that is the total ARR from all 32 upgrades. The genuinely incremental ARR is $224,000 before accounting for churn, rollout timing, and margin. There is a second structural issue: the case assumes a 20% reduction in time-to-quote will cause conversion to increase, but it provides no evidence connecting those two outcomes.
- **Is the kill criterion complete and actionable? Does it name the consequence, or hand the decision back to the room?:** It names a consequence, but it isn’t fully actionable. The kill criterion says: If Standard-to-Enterprise upsell has not reached 7% by the end of Q3, the AI estimation feature is paused and the Q4 engineering capacity reallocated before headcount is committed. What holds up: - Clear threshold: 7% conversion. - Clear deadline: End of Q3. - A stated consequence: Pause the feature and reallocate Q4 engineering capacity. What’s missing: 1. No clear owner: Who decides and executes the pause and reallocation? 2. The metric may be misleading: Conversion could reach 7% without the feature causing the improvement. Conversely, it might miss 7% because there hasn’t been enough time or enough eligible customers to measure the effect reliably. 3. “Reallocated” is vague: It doesn’t say who chooses the alternative work or where the capacity goes. 4. No explicit decision rule: Is the feature automatically paused at below 7%, or does the team discuss whether to pause it?
- **Your verdict: FUND / FUND WITH ONE CONDITION / DO NOT FUND. If a condition, name it; otherwise explain in one sentence.:** FUND WITH ONE CONDITION: Validate that the feature can drive genuinely incremental Standard-to-Enterprise conversion from 6% to 8% before committing the full $180,000 build, using a measurable pilot or credible control group and calculating payback on incremental contribution margin not total ARR.
